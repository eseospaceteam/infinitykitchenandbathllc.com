Bathroom Design Guide — Avondale, AZ

Bathroom Flooring Options: What Works Best in Arizona's Climate

By Steve Hunt, Owner & Lead Designer at Infinity Kitchens and Baths

Updated November 20, 2026 • 11 min read

Part of our Bathroom Design & Planning Guides — see all 8 guides in this category.

Reviewed by Steve Hunt, Owner & Lead Designer · AZ ROC #339999 · Last reviewed November 20, 2026

Quick answer: For most Arizona bathrooms, the best flooring is porcelain tile in a matte or textured finish: it absorbs 0.5% water or less, never needs sealing, and shrugs off hard-water cleaning. Rigid-core luxury vinyl plank is the warmer, lower-cost waterproof option outside the shower. Natural stone looks beautiful but needs regular sealing, and ceramic tile suits lighter-use guest baths.

Bathroom flooring selection involves a short but unforgiving list of requirements: moisture resistance, slip resistance, durability, and comfort underfoot. Arizona's climate adds two more variables: extreme heat and hard water. This guide covers the flooring options we actually install in bathrooms and the considerations that typically drive the decision.

Avondale Climate Factors

Before comparing materials, a few climate factors that matter for bathroom flooring in Avondale:

  • Heat: Avondale is low desert, about 1,000 feet. National Weather Service 1991–2020 normals for the area put the July average high near 106°F, with about 111 days a year at 100°F or hotter. Flooring materials that expand and contract significantly (some LVP products, hardwood) need proper acclimation and expansion gaps, especially near sunny windows.
  • Hard water: Avondale's tap water is a blend of city well water and Colorado River (CAP) water, and it is very hard: the city's 2022 water quality report put city-produced water at an average of about 21 grains per gallon (357 mg/L). High calcium and magnesium content means mineral deposits (calcium scale) form on grout lines and light-colored tile surfaces faster than in soft-water regions. This affects both material selection (lighter grout shows scale more) and cleaning requirements.
  • Dry climate baseline: Despite the monsoon season (officially June 15 through September 30), Avondale's baseline humidity is low most of the year. This works in favor of natural wood and some LVP products that could be problematic in high-humidity coastal markets.

Material Comparison

Porcelain Tile

Porcelain tile is the most versatile and durable bathroom flooring option available. Under the ANSI A137.1 standard, a tile only counts as porcelain if it absorbs 0.5% water or less, so the tile itself won't soak up water. It doesn't expand or contract meaningfully with temperature change, resists staining without sealing, and is available in virtually every aesthetic (stone looks, wood looks, concrete, geometric patterns). Slip resistance varies by finish: matte and textured surfaces grip better than polished ones. Check the tile's wet DCOF rating, explained in the slip resistance section below.

The trade-offs: it's the most labor-intensive to install (requires cement board underlayment, thinset, grout), it's the hardest underfoot (standing on tile for long periods is less comfortable than other options), and it's the coldest to step on first thing in the morning — an issue addressed by radiant heat under the tile.

Cost range installed: $8–$20/sq ft for standard porcelain; $14–$30/sq ft for premium large-format or specialty tile.

Luxury Vinyl Plank (LVP)

LVP has taken significant market share in bathroom flooring over the past decade for good reason: rigid-core planks are sold as 100% waterproof, they're comfortable underfoot (softer and warmer than tile), easy to install, and available in convincing wood-look formats. It's also significantly easier to replace than tile if a section is damaged.

"Waterproof" describes the plank, not the room. A floating floor needs a gap at the walls to expand: MSI's bathroom installation guide, for example, calls for a 1/4-inch gap around the perimeter and says vinyl edges exposed to the toilet flange or shower edge must be sealed with silicone caulk. Those edges are where an overflowing toilet or tub sends water under the floor, so they're the ones that need careful sealing.

The considerations specific to Arizona: vinyl expands and contracts with temperature, so in direct sun or rooms that heat up, some LVP products can buckle if installed without adequate expansion gaps. For an interior bathroom floor out of direct sun, this is rarely a problem. Follow the specific product's temperature range and acclimation instructions, which vary by manufacturer.

LVP is appropriate for bathroom floors outside the shower area (the main floor, around the toilet, under the vanity). We don't recommend LVP for shower floors — the shower floor needs tile with proper slope and drain integration.

Cost range installed: $5–$12/sq ft for standard LVP; $8–$16/sq ft for premium thicker-core products.

Natural Stone (Travertine, Slate, Limestone)

Natural stone bathroom floors create a visual warmth and authenticity that no manufactured product fully replicates. Travertine, in particular, is extremely popular in Arizona homes — it reads warm and organic rather than cool and modern, and it's been used in the Southwest long enough to feel at home in desert architecture.

The maintenance reality in a hard-water market: natural stone requires sealing at installation and annually thereafter. Travertine's open pores absorb water, soap, and calcium scale if left unsealed. In Avondale's very hard water, unsealed travertine in a bathroom picks up white calcium staining quickly. If sealed and maintained, it lasts for decades and looks better with age.

Slate is more dense and less porous than travertine — a lower-maintenance natural stone option with a rougher texture that provides natural slip resistance.

Cost range installed: $10–$25/sq ft for travertine or slate; $18–$40/sq ft for premium marble.

Ceramic Tile

Ceramic tile is a step below porcelain in density and water resistance (higher water absorption rate, more susceptible to cracking under impact), but it's significantly more affordable and appropriate for bathroom floors in lower-traffic bathrooms, guest baths, or areas where budget is the primary driver. Choose a ceramic rated for floors, not a wall tile. On shower floors, porcelain is the professional standard because of its low absorption; our ceramic vs. porcelain tile guide explains the difference in detail.

Cost range installed: $5–$12/sq ft.

Slip Resistance: What the DCOF Rating Means for a Bathroom Floor

Bathroom floors get wet, so slip resistance deserves as much attention as looks. Tile makers publish a wet DCOF (dynamic coefficient of friction) number measured under the ANSI A326.3 test. The higher the number, the more grip when the floor is wet.

Wet DCOFWhat it meansWhere it fits in a home bathroom
Under 0.42Below the ANSI A137.1 minimum for level interior floors walked on when wetWalls, or floors that stay dry
0.42 or higherMeets the ANSI A137.1 minimum; Daltile classes it Interior Wet, with toilet facilities as an exampleThe main bathroom floor
0.60 or higherDaltile's Interior Wet Plus class, for areas with constant water and light or no footwear, such as public showersShower floors, curbless showers, steam showers

Thresholds: ANSI A137.1 and Daltile's DCOF product use classifications.

Two cautions from the manufacturer's own guidance: no floor tile is "slip proof," and a DCOF number alone shouldn't be read as a guarantee that a slip can't happen. Finish matters as much as the number: matte and textured surfaces are the safer choice on floors, so save polished tile for walls. For a barrier-free bath, our curbless shower design guide covers how the floor slopes to the drain.

Waterproof vs. Water-Resistant Bathroom Flooring

These two labels get used loosely, and the difference decides what belongs in a bathroom. Flooring America puts it simply: water-resistant flooring holds off water for a little while, but if water sits too long it can swell or warp, while waterproof flooring won't let water soak through no matter how long it sits.

  • Waterproof as a material: porcelain tile and rigid-core vinyl plank.
  • Water-resistant only: laminate, which Flooring America says is "not ideal if your bathroom sees frequent soaking or splashes," and engineered hardwood, which handles humidity better than solid wood but is still vulnerable to standing water.
  • Not a waterproof system on its own: tile and cement grout. Under a shower floor, the waterproofing is the liner or membrane beneath the tile, which is why Avondale's shower inspections include a pre-slope and a shower liner test. Our Avondale permits guide lists those inspections.

Radiant Heat Under Bathroom Floors

Tile's most significant comfort drawback — cold underfoot — is solved by electric radiant heat under the tile. A thermostat-controlled heating mat is installed below the tile during the bathroom remodel. The result is a warm tile floor that responds within 15–20 minutes of activation. Avondale's winters are mild, but January lows still average in the 40s, so tile can feel cold first thing on a winter morning. Here radiant heat is a comfort upgrade rather than a necessity, at a one-time installation cost of $600–$1,500 depending on floor area.

Radiant heat works with tile and natural stone. Vinyl can work too, with limits: MSI notes that most vinyl flooring is fine over radiant heat, that many manufacturers cap the floor at 85°F (MSI recommends staying below 80°F), that a floating click-lock plank is the type to use, and that you should confirm the heat won't void the plank's warranty. Check the specific product's limits before pairing the two.

Grout Considerations for Avondale's Hard Water

Grout color choice matters more with water as hard as Avondale's than in most places, because of the calcium scale it leaves behind. The practical guidance:

  • Avoid white or very light grout on floors: Calcium deposits are white-ish. On a white grout floor, the buildup is nearly invisible until it's a thick layer. On medium grey grout, regular cleaning removes it before it accumulates.
  • Epoxy grout is worth the premium: Epoxy grout is non-porous — calcium scale sits on the surface rather than absorbing in. Much easier to clean than cement grout in hard water areas.
  • Sealed cement grout is second choice: If epoxy isn't in the budget, seal cement grout at installation and annually. Medium grey or tan colors hide buildup better than white.

Frequently Asked Questions

What type of flooring is best for a bathroom?

Porcelain tile with a matte or textured finish and a wet DCOF of at least 0.42 is the best all-around bathroom floor: it absorbs 0.5% water or less, needs no sealing, and lasts. Rigid-core LVP is the best lower-cost choice outside the shower if you want something warmer underfoot.

What are the most regretted bathroom flooring choices?

The choices most likely to disappoint are water-resistant floors such as laminate in a bath that gets splashed, polished tile on the floor where it's slippery when wet, and natural stone that isn't resealed in hard water. Each one fails for a predictable reason, so match the floor to how the bathroom is actually used. For a closer look at the two most common picks, see LVP vs. tile in bathrooms.

Can I put LVP in a bathroom with a shower?

Yes — on the main bathroom floor around the shower, under the vanity, and around the toilet. Not inside the shower floor, where tile with proper slope and drain integration is required. The transition between the LVP bathroom floor and the tile shower floor is typically handled with a threshold or flush transition strip.

Is heated tile flooring worth it in Avondale?

It can be, if you spend meaningful time barefoot in your bathroom on winter mornings. Avondale's winters are mild, but National Weather Service 1991–2020 normals for the area still put January lows in the 40s, so tile can feel cold in the cooler months. Expect to use it a few months a year, not half the year. The installation cost ($600–$1,500) is modest relative to the comfort benefit, and a timer or thermostat keeps it running only when you want it.

What's the best bathroom floor for resale value?

Porcelain tile in a neutral color (white, grey, greige, or a warm wood-look) has the broadest appeal and the longest recognized lifespan. LVP is increasingly accepted and recognized by buyers as a quality choice in secondary bathrooms. Natural stone reads as premium but is noted by buyers as requiring maintenance. For maximum appeal at resale, porcelain in a neutral format is the consistent answer.

How long does bathroom flooring installation take?

Tile removal and new porcelain tile installation in an average primary bathroom (60–80 sq ft) takes 2–3 days including subfloor prep, setting, and grout cure time. LVP installation is faster — typically 4–6 hours once the subfloor is prepped. If radiant heat is added, add 4–6 hours for the mat installation and thermostat wiring before tile goes down.
