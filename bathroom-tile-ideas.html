Bathroom Tile Ideas: Floors, Showers & Walls

By Steve Hunt, Owner & Lead Designer at Infinity Kitchens and Baths

Updated July 1, 2026 • 12 min read

Part of our Shower & Tile Guides — see all 4 guides in this category.

Quick answer: The best bathroom tile choices come down to material and location. Use dense, water-resistant porcelain for floors and shower pans, prioritizing slip resistance (a matte or textured tile with a wet DCOF of 0.42 or higher). Reach for glazed ceramic on walls, where it's affordable and easy to clean, and save natural stone and glass mosaics for accent walls, niches, and feature areas. Pick a tile size and layout that suits the room — large-format tile opens up small baths, while classic subway, herringbone, and vertical-stack layouts add character. Then let the grout color and a professional, well-waterproofed installation tie it all together.

Tile is the hardest-working design element in any bathroom. It shrugs off water and steam, wipes clean, and — done well — turns an ordinary bath into the room people compliment first. But "what tile should I use?" is really several questions at once: which material, what size, which layout, and where each belongs on the floor, walls, and shower. Get those right and the room looks intentional and lasts for decades. Get them wrong and you end up with a slippery floor, cracked grout, or a busy pattern that fights itself.

This guide walks through the tile decisions we make with homeowners across Prescott, the Quad Cities, and the Verde Valley — the materials worth knowing, the sizes and trends shaping bathrooms right now, the layouts that never go out of style, and the practical details (slip resistance, niches, curbless showers, grout, and mixing tiles) that separate a polished result from a regretted one. Use it to plan with confidence whether you're refreshing a powder room or gutting a primary bath.

1. Bathroom Tile Materials and Where Each Fits

Every tile decision starts with the material, because that determines how the tile handles water, wear, and cleaning. Here are the surfaces you'll actually be choosing between, and where each earns its place.

Porcelain

Porcelain is the workhorse of the bathroom. It's clay fired at a very high temperature, which makes it dense, extremely hard, and nearly impervious to water. That combination makes it the safest all-around choice for floors and shower pans, and it's just as at home on walls. Better still, porcelain comes in convincing wood-look planks and stone-look slabs, so you can get the warmth of oak or the drama of marble without any of the maintenance. If you only remember one rule, make it this: when in doubt on a bathroom floor, choose porcelain.

Ceramic

Ceramic is porcelain's more affordable cousin — also clay-based, but fired at a lower temperature, so it's a bit softer and more porous. Glazed ceramic is a fantastic value for walls, wainscot, and accent areas where it won't take heavy foot traffic. Classic white subway tile is ceramic, and it remains one of the most versatile, budget-friendly wall tiles you can buy. For a deeper comparison of the two, see our guide on tile and custom shower installation.

Natural stone

Marble, travertine, slate, and limestone bring texture and one-of-a-kind character no manufactured tile can copy. The trade-off is maintenance: natural stone is porous and needs periodic sealing to resist water spotting and stains — a real consideration in Prescott's hard-water areas. Polished stone can also be slick underfoot, so it's usually smarter on walls and accents than on floors, unless it's honed or textured. Love the look but not the upkeep? Stone-look porcelain gets you most of the way there with far less fuss.

Glass

Glass tile reflects light beautifully, resists water and staining completely, and adds a jewel-like shimmer that's perfect for accent bands, niches, and feature walls. It's typically too slick and too costly to cover an entire floor, but as a strip of color or a shower niche liner it delivers a lot of impact for a small quantity.

Mosaic

Mosaics are small tiles — often 2 inches or less — mounted on mesh sheets. Because they pack in so many grout lines, they grip better underfoot, which makes them the go-to for shower floors where traction matters and the tile needs to follow the slope to the drain. They also shine as accent inlays, niche backers, and decorative borders. Penny rounds, hexagons, and tiny squares all fall in this family.

2. Tile Sizes and the Large-Format Trend

Size changes the whole feel of a room as much as color does. Bathroom tile generally falls into a few buckets: mosaics (2 inches and under), standard field tile (roughly 4x4 to 6x6), rectangular subway (3x6, 4x12), and large-format tile (12x24, 24x24, and up to true slab panels).

The clear trend right now is large-format. Big tiles mean fewer grout lines, which reads as a cleaner, more continuous, more contemporary surface — and, importantly, less grout to scrub over the years. In a small bathroom, large-format tile actually makes the room feel bigger because the eye isn't chopped up by a grid of joints. Running the same large tile from the floor up the shower walls, or extending floor tile straight into a curbless shower, blurs the boundaries and stretches the space visually.

That said, size should follow function. Large tiles are wonderful on floors and walls but a poor fit for a shower pan, where the tile has to slope to the drain — that's where small mosaics still win. Match the tile scale to the surface, and don't be afraid to mix sizes on purpose (large on the walls, mosaic on the shower floor) for both looks and safety.

3. Popular Tile Layouts and Patterns

Two homeowners can use the exact same tile and end up with completely different rooms based on layout alone. These are the patterns that consistently look great in bathrooms:

  • Subway / offset (running bond). The timeless brick-style layout with each row offset by half. A safe, clean classic — traditional at a 50% offset, more modern at a 30% offset.
  • Herringbone. Rectangular tiles set in a zig-zag for a dynamic, upscale look. Beautiful on floors and feature walls; it uses more material and labor because of the angled cuts.
  • Vertical stack. Tiles stacked in straight columns rather than offset. Crisp, modern, and great for making a ceiling feel taller — vertical subway tile is having a real moment.
  • Chevron. Similar to herringbone but the tiles are mitered so the zig-zag forms continuous, pointed rows. Bold and elegant, ideal for an accent wall.
  • Straight stack / grid. Tiles aligned in a simple square grid — understated, contemporary, and perfect for large-format tile where you want the material, not the pattern, to star.

A reliable approach is to keep the field tile calm and let one layout do the talking — a herringbone shower floor, say, framed by simple stacked wall tile. For a gallery of shower-specific combinations, browse our tile shower ideas.

4. Choosing Floor vs. Wall vs. Shower Tile

Not all tile is rated for all locations, and the differences matter. Here's how to think about each surface.

Floor tile

Floors need to be hard, water-resistant, and — above all — slip-resistant. Porcelain is the standard pick. Look for tile specifically rated for floor use (wall-only tile is often too soft) and favor matte or textured finishes over high-gloss, which turns treacherous when wet. Wood-look porcelain planks are a huge favorite in bathrooms because they give a warm, spa-like feel without wood's vulnerability to water.

Wall tile

Walls take no foot traffic, so you have more freedom. This is the place for glossy finishes, delicate glass, and softer ceramics that wouldn't survive a floor. Wall tile can also climb higher than you might expect — a full-height tile wall behind the vanity or around the tub adds drama and protects the surface from splashes.

Shower tile

The shower is the most demanding zone in the house: constant water, steam, and the need for traction underfoot. Use porcelain or glazed ceramic on the walls and small textured mosaics on the pan. What lies beneath the tile matters even more than the tile itself — a properly waterproofed substrate is what keeps water out of your walls. If you'd rather skip grout maintenance entirely, our custom shower installation team can also walk you through groutless systems.

5. Slip Resistance: What COF and DCOF Mean

Slip resistance isn't a design afterthought — it's a safety spec you should check before you buy floor or shower tile. Manufacturers publish a coefficient of friction (COF), and the number that matters for wet bathrooms is the dynamic coefficient of friction (DCOF), measured under wet conditions. The widely used industry benchmark is a wet DCOF of 0.42 or higher for floors expected to get wet.

A few practical takeaways: matte and textured tiles almost always outperform polished ones; smaller tiles and mosaics add traction because the extra grout lines break up the surface; and glossy stone or high-shine porcelain, however gorgeous, belongs on walls, not on a wet floor. On the shower pan specifically, small mosaics are the traction champion. If the tile's DCOF isn't listed, ask — a reputable supplier will have the number.

6. Accent Walls, Niches, and Curbless Showers

The details are where a tiled bathroom goes from nice to memorable.

Accent walls. Devote one wall — behind the tub, the vanity, or inside the shower — to a standout tile: a bold geometric, a natural-stone slab-look, or a shimmering glass mosaic. Keeping the accent to a single plane lets you splurge on a beautiful, pricier tile without overwhelming the room or the budget.

Niches. A recessed shower niche is both practical storage and a design opportunity. Line it in a contrasting mosaic or the same tile as an accent wall, and align it with your grout lines so it looks built-in rather than cut-in. Building the niche between studs during the remodel is far cleaner than adding a surface caddy later.

Curbless showers. A curbless (zero-threshold) shower — where the floor tile runs continuously into the shower with no step over a curb — looks sleek, feels spacious, and is a cornerstone of accessible, aging-in-place design. It requires careful sloping and waterproofing to keep water headed toward the drain, so it's very much a job for an experienced installer, but the payoff in both style and safety is significant.

7. Grout Color and Maintenance

Grout is easy to treat as an afterthought, but it dramatically changes both the look and the upkeep of your tile. Two strategies dominate:

  • Matching grout (close to the tile color) makes the surface read as one continuous plane, hides minor layout imperfections, and helps a small room feel larger. It's the go-to for large-format tile and calm, modern spaces.
  • Contrasting grout (dark grout with white tile, for example) celebrates the pattern and gives subway or geometric layouts a graphic punch. Because it outlines every joint, though, the installation has to be precise.

For maintenance, lean toward a slightly darker grout on floors and shower pans, where a bright white will show grime fast. Even better, choose a high-performance or epoxy grout in wet areas — it resists staining and water far better than standard cement grout and stays looking new with less scrubbing. Sealing standard grout periodically also goes a long way in Prescott's hard water.

8. Mixing Tiles Cohesively

Combining several tiles is how designers create depth, but it's also where amateur jobs go sideways. A few rules keep a mix feeling intentional rather than chaotic:

  • Vary the scale, not everything. Pair a large field tile with a small mosaic accent so the sizes contrast clearly instead of competing.
  • Let one tile lead. Choose a single hero — the shower accent or the floor — and keep the rest quiet so the eye has somewhere to rest.
  • Share an undertone. Tiles in different materials read as a set when they share a common color temperature (all warm or all cool).
  • Limit the palette. Two or three tiles in one bathroom is plenty; beyond that, the room starts to feel busy.
  • Sample in the actual room. Lighting shifts color dramatically — always look at samples on-site, in the bathroom's own light, before committing.

Tile Selection Tips

  • Buy 10–15% extra tile for cuts, waste, and future repairs — and keep the leftovers with matching lot numbers.
  • Confirm floor and shower tiles are rated for those uses and meet a wet DCOF of at least 0.42.
  • Order all tile for a room from the same production lot to avoid subtle shade variation.
  • Favor matte and textured finishes underfoot; save glossy tile for walls.
  • Choose large-format tile to open up a small bath, mosaics where you need grip.
  • Pick your grout color deliberately — it's as much a design decision as the tile.
  • Plan niches, accent walls, and curbless thresholds before demo, not after.
  • Prioritize the waterproofing system in showers; it's what protects the whole installation.

Why Professional Tile Installation Matters

Tile is only as good as what's under it. Beautiful tile over a poorly waterproofed or uneven substrate will eventually crack, leak, or grow mold — and by the time you see the damage, it's usually behind the wall. Professional installation covers the parts you'll never see but absolutely feel over time: correct waterproofing membranes in wet areas, a dead-level and properly prepared substrate, consistent grout lines and spacing, precise cuts around niches and fixtures, and an accurate slope to the drain so water always leaves the shower.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best tile for a bathroom floor?

Porcelain is the best all-around bathroom floor tile: it's dense, nearly waterproof, and holds up to daily traffic. For floors, prioritize slip resistance — look for a textured or matte tile with a wet dynamic coefficient of friction (DCOF) of at least 0.42, and consider smaller tiles or mosaics where more grout lines add grip. Porcelain wood-look planks and stone-look tiles give you the warmth of wood or stone without the water and maintenance worries.

What tile is best for a shower?

Porcelain and glazed ceramic are the top choices for shower walls because they're water-resistant and easy to clean. On the shower floor, use small mosaic tiles (2 inches or smaller) or a textured tile — the extra grout lines create traction and let the tile follow the slope to the drain. Natural stone like marble looks stunning in a shower but needs sealing to resist water and staining. A properly waterproofed substrate matters more than the tile itself.

Porcelain vs. ceramic tile — what's the difference?

Both are made from clay and fired, but porcelain is fired hotter and denser, which makes it harder, less porous, and better suited to floors and wet areas. Ceramic is a bit softer and more affordable, making it a great value for walls and lower-traffic areas. For bathroom floors and shower pans, porcelain is usually the safer pick; for accent walls and wainscot, ceramic can stretch your budget without sacrificing looks.

What tile size makes a small bathroom look bigger?

Large-format tiles (12x24, 24x24, or bigger) make a small bathroom feel more open because fewer grout lines create a cleaner, more continuous surface. Running the same tile from floor up the walls, or extending floor tile into a curbless shower, blurs boundaries and stretches the space visually. Light colors and a grout that closely matches the tile also help a small room read larger and calmer.

What grout color should I choose for bathroom tile?

Matching the grout to the tile creates a seamless, expansive look and hides minor imperfections — ideal for large-format tile and small rooms. A contrasting grout highlights the tile pattern and is popular with subway and geometric layouts, but it draws the eye to every line, so installation has to be precise. On floors and shower pans, a slightly darker grout hides dirt better. Epoxy or high-performance grout resists staining and is worth it in wet areas.

Are natural stone tiles a good idea in a bathroom?

Natural stone like marble, travertine, and slate is beautiful and adds real character, but it's porous and needs periodic sealing to resist water spots and staining — something to weigh in Prescott's hard-water areas. Polished stone can be slippery underfoot, so it's better on walls than floors unless it's honed or textured. If you love the look but want less maintenance, stone-look porcelain gives you the aesthetic with far easier care.

Should tile installation be left to a professional?

Yes — especially in showers and wet areas. Tile is only as good as the substrate and waterproofing beneath it, and a hidden failure can lead to leaks, mold, and costly damage down the road. A professional handles proper waterproofing, dead-level layout, consistent grout lines, precise cuts around niches and fixtures, and correct slope to the drain.
