Bathroom Design Guide — Prescott, AZ

Best Bathroom Storage Ideas (2026)

By Steve Hunt, Owner & Lead Designer at Infinity Kitchens and Baths

Updated September 22, 2026 • 9 min read

Quick answer: The best bathroom storage comes from building into the walls and upward instead of out onto the floor. Start with a vanity that uses drawers instead of doors, add a recessed medicine cabinet and a recessed shower niche, then use tall, narrow cabinetry such as a linen tower or over-toilet cabinet for bulk items. Plan it all during a remodel, when walls are open. Our bathroom vanities page shows the vanity options we install.

Most bathrooms do not lack square footage so much as they waste it. A sink base with two doors and one shelf, a surface-mounted mirror, and a wire caddy in the shower are three storage decisions that each leave usable space on the table.

These are the storage ideas we recommend most in Prescott and West Valley bathroom remodels, ordered by how much they change daily use. A few, like recessed cabinets and niches, only make sense while the walls are open. That is the main reason to decide storage early in a bathroom remodel, before tile and drywall lock in the layout.

The list

  1. Vanity Drawers Instead of Doors
  2. Recessed Medicine Cabinet
  3. Linen Tower or Side Cabinet
  4. Over-Toilet Cabinet
  5. Recessed Shower Niche
  6. Floating Vanity With Drawers
  7. Drawer Organizers and Pull-Outs
  8. Double Vanity or a Long Single Vanity
  9. In-Drawer Outlet (Electrician and Permit Required)

1 Vanity Drawers Instead of Doors

Best single storage upgrade

If you change one thing, make the vanity mostly drawers. Deep drawers keep everyday items visible and reachable, while a door-and-shelf base turns into a pile you dig through on your knees. Drawers also hide the clutter that makes a bathroom look old fast.

Doors still have a place. Tall bottles, a hair dryer, and cleaning supplies fit better behind a door than in a shallow drawer. A good split is drawers on the sides with a door or U-shaped drawer at the sink, where the drain trap sits. Our bathroom vanity guide walks through the configurations.

Pros

  • Everything visible at a glance
  • No kneeling to reach the back

Watch-outs

  • Drawer under the sink must be notched around plumbing

2 Recessed Medicine Cabinet

Best for small bathrooms

A medicine cabinet set into the wall cavity between the studs adds real storage with essentially zero footprint, and it doubles as your mirror. It is one of the few upgrades that makes a small bath feel both bigger and more organized at the same time.

Feasibility depends on the wall. Before we confirm a recessed unit, we check the wall cavity depth and look for obstructions such as plumbing vents, supply lines, and wiring. If the wall behind the vanity is a plumbing wall, a surface-mounted cabinet or a recessed unit on a side wall may be the better answer. Either way, a medicine cabinet above the vanity roughly doubles usable storage at the sink.

Pros

  • Storage with no floor or counter footprint
  • Replaces the mirror

Watch-outs

  • Wall must be clear of pipes and wiring

3 Linen Tower or Side Cabinet

Best for towels and bulk supplies

A tall linen tower beside the vanity handles what the vanity cannot: towels, extra toilet paper, and backup supplies. In a primary bath with a long vanity wall, a tower at one end can match the vanity cabinetry so it reads as one built-in piece.

The caution is scale. The most common small-bath mistake is trying to fit a full tub, a separate shower, a double vanity, and a linen tower into a room with space for two of them. A slim tower is worth it if the width allows; if it pinches the walkway, skip it and put that storage into the vanity and the walls instead.

Pros

  • Holds bulky items the vanity cannot
  • Can match vanity cabinetry

Watch-outs

  • Needs spare wall width

4 Over-Toilet Cabinet

Best use of dead wall space

The wall above the toilet is usually empty. A shallow wall cabinet there uses vertical space that would otherwise go to waste and keeps spare supplies close without cluttering the counter.

Keep it shallow and high enough that nobody hits their head standing at the toilet, and anchor it into studs or blocking. Closed doors look calmer than open shelves in a small room; open shelves are fine for rolled towels if you like the look and do not mind dusting. In a remodel we match the finish to the vanity so it looks planned rather than bought separately.

Pros

  • Uses space nothing else can
  • Inexpensive to include in a remodel

Watch-outs

  • Must be shallow to stay out of the way

5 Recessed Shower Niche

Best for shower storage

A tiled niche built into the shower wall holds bottles without a protruding caddy and gives you a clean spot for an accent tile. One larger niche is more useful than several small ones, and a shelf inside the opening doubles the tiers.

The niche must sit inside the shower’s waterproofing, with its bottom pitched so water drains out, which is why it has to be planned before the walls are closed. Our shower niche and bench ideas page covers sizing, placement, and how the niche fits between studs.

Pros

  • No caddy, no clutter
  • Works in any size shower

Watch-outs

  • Only practical during a shower remodel

6 Floating Vanity With Drawers

Best for making a small bath feel bigger

A wall-mounted vanity leaves the floor visible underneath, which makes a small room read larger and makes mopping easier. With drawers instead of doors, it can still hold a surprising amount.

The trade-off is that a floating vanity gives slightly less storage than a floor-standing one of the same width, because there is no toe kick or base below. It also needs solid wall framing; we typically add a horizontal ledger or blocking between the studs to carry the load. A floating vanity lets you choose the height instead of accepting the standard 32-inch floor-mounted height. See the styles on our bathroom vanities page.

Standard floor-mounted height32 in.
SupportLedger or blocking between studs

Pros

  • Open floor looks larger
  • Height set to suit you

Watch-outs

  • Slightly less storage than floor-standing
  • Needs blocking in the wall

7 Drawer Organizers and Pull-Outs

Best low-cost add-on

A deep drawer without dividers becomes a junk drawer. Drawer dividers, a pull-out shelf in a door base, and a tray for small items turn the same cabinet into organized storage. They cost little when ordered with the vanity and are awkward to retrofit to odd drawer sizes later.

Think about who uses which drawer. In a shared primary bath, each person getting their own stack of drawers usually ends more arguments than a larger mirror does.

Pros

  • Cheap when specified up front
  • Makes deep drawers usable

8 Double Vanity or a Long Single Vanity

Best for shared primary baths

In a primary bath, a double vanity at 60 to 72 inches or more gives each person a sink and their own storage. Keep at least a 30-inch clear path between fixtures so the room still works.

If the wall is shorter, a longer single vanity with more drawers and counter can be more useful than squeezing in two sinks, especially if only one person uses the bathroom at a time. Our master bathroom ideas guide covers the layout side, and small bathroom ideas covers the opposite problem.

9 In-Drawer Outlet (Electrician and Permit Required)

Best for hair tools, if you plan it properly

An outlet inside a vanity drawer lets a hair dryer or trimmer stay plugged in and out of sight. It is a nice idea, but it is electrical work, not cabinetry. It needs a licensed electrician, GFCI protection, a product listed for in-drawer use, and in most cases a permit and inspection as part of the remodel.

Treat this as something to raise at design, not a gadget to add later. Running a new circuit or relocating a box is straightforward while the walls are open and difficult once they are closed. If the electrical work is not in scope, a drawer with a heat-safe liner for tools is a simpler option.

Pros

  • Tools stay plugged in and hidden

Watch-outs

  • Requires a licensed electrician and usually a permit
  • Must use a listed in-drawer product

Plan Storage Before Demolition

The storage ideas that make the biggest difference live inside walls: a recessed medicine cabinet, a shower niche, blocking for a floating vanity, and wiring for an in-drawer outlet. All of them are straightforward when the walls are open and expensive or impossible once they are closed. At Infinity Kitchens and Baths we design storage alongside the layout during the free in-home consultation, then build it in during rough-in. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best way to add storage to a small bathroom?

Build into the walls and upward instead of onto the floor. A recessed medicine cabinet and a recessed shower niche add storage with no footprint, a floating vanity with drawers keeps everyday items organized, and a slim linen tower or over-toilet cabinet uses vertical space that would otherwise go to waste.

Are drawers or doors better in a bathroom vanity?

Drawers are better for most everyday items because everything is visible and reachable without kneeling. Doors still work for tall bottles, a hair dryer, and cleaning supplies. A common layout is drawer stacks on the sides with a door or U-shaped drawer at the sink to clear the plumbing.

Can any wall take a recessed medicine cabinet?

No. The wall cavity has to be deep enough and free of obstructions such as plumbing vents, supply lines, and wiring. We check the wall before confirming a recessed unit. If the wall behind the sink is a plumbing wall, a side wall or a surface-mounted cabinet may be the better choice.

Do floating vanities have less storage?

Slightly less than a floor-standing vanity of the same width, because there is no base below the cabinet. Choosing drawers instead of doors makes up much of the difference. A floating vanity also needs solid blocking or a ledger in the wall, which is easiest to add during a remodel.

Can I put an outlet inside a bathroom drawer?

It can be done, but it is electrical work that needs a licensed electrician, GFCI protection, a product listed for in-drawer use, and usually a permit and inspection. It is best planned during a bathroom remodel while the walls are open, not added afterward.
