Best Cabinet Hardware Styles (2026)

By Steve Hunt, Owner & Lead Designer at Infinity Kitchens and Baths

Updated September 22, 2026 • 8 min read

Quick answer: The best all-around cabinet hardware is a simple bar pull: it suits shaker and slab doors, it is easy to grip, and it works in every finish. Cup pulls and knobs suit traditional and farmhouse kitchens, while edge pulls and integrated or handleless fronts suit modern flat-panel cabinets. For finishes in 2026, warm brass and bronze are gaining on matte black and satin nickel. A common rule of thumb is a pull about one-third the width of the drawer.

Hardware is the jewelry of a kitchen, and it is also the part you touch dozens of times a day. It is one of the cheaper pieces of a remodel and one of the easiest to change later, which is exactly why our 2026 trends guide suggests putting your trend-forward choices here rather than in the cabinets or counters.

Below are the hardware styles we install most, ranked by how widely they work. The sizing notes are rules of thumb that designers use, not fixed standards; your door style and drawer sizes decide the final pick. Hardware goes on last in a remodel, after everything else is in place.

The list

  1. Bar Pulls
  2. Cup (Bin) Pulls
  3. Round Knobs
  4. Knob-and-Pull Mix
  5. Edge (Tab) Pulls
  6. Integrated Channel (Handleless)
  7. Push-to-Open Hardware
  8. Appliance Pulls

1 Bar Pulls

Best all-around choice for most kitchens

A straight bar on two posts. It is the most versatile pull there is: on white shaker it reads clean and timeless, on slab doors it reads modern, and in brass on a green or navy cabinet it reads tailored. Bars are also easy to grab with a full hand, which matters for anyone with stiff fingers.

Sizing is where bar pulls go right or wrong. The rule of thumb is a pull about one-third the width of the drawer, and on very wide drawers either a longer bar or two pulls. Common center-to-center sizes include 3 inches, 96 mm, 128 mm and 160 mm, so decide on one family of sizes before you order. Our door style guide pairs bar pulls with both shaker and flat panel.

Pros

  • Works with almost any door style
  • Easy full-hand grip
  • Available in every finish and length

Watch-outs

  • Sizing across many drawer widths takes planning

2 Cup (Bin) Pulls

Best for farmhouse, traditional and craftsman drawers

A half-moon cup you hook your fingers under. Cup pulls are the classic drawer pull for farmhouse and traditional kitchens, and they look especially good on stacked drawer banks. They are a natural pairing for shaker and raised-panel doors.

They belong on drawers, not doors; the usual approach is cup pulls on drawers with knobs or small bar pulls on the doors. Cups have a small grip opening, so try them in person, and be aware that the inside of the cup collects crumbs in a baking zone.

Pros

  • Classic, warm look
  • Great on drawer banks

Watch-outs

  • Tighter grip; harder with arthritis
  • Drawers only

3 Round Knobs

Best budget-friendly choice for cabinet doors

A single-post knob is the simplest hardware there is: easy to install, small, and usually the least expensive per piece. Knobs are a traditional choice for doors, and small knobs on upper doors keep a kitchen from looking cluttered with hardware.

The downside is grip. Turning or pinching a small knob is harder for aging hands than pulling a bar, and the 2010 ADA Standards (§309.4) call for operable parts that work with one hand without tight grasping, pinching or twisting of the wrist. If anyone in the house has arthritis, lean toward pulls; see our aging-in-place remodeling page.

Pros

  • Simple and economical
  • Low visual clutter on doors

Watch-outs

  • Harder to grip than a pull
  • Can look dated on large drawers

4 Knob-and-Pull Mix

Best for transitional kitchens

The most common real-world scheme: knobs on doors, pulls on drawers, in one matching finish. It gives a kitchen rhythm, uses the more economical knob where it works well, and puts the easier-to-use pull on drawers, which you open far more and which carry more weight.

Keep it to one finish and one design family so the mix looks intentional. If you want a single style everywhere, bar pulls on everything is the other clean option. Either way, lay the hardware out on the CAD drawing so every door and drawer is accounted for before you order.

Pros

  • Balanced, classic look
  • Pull where it matters most

Watch-outs

  • Two part numbers to match and stock

5 Edge (Tab) Pulls

Best for slim, modern flat-panel cabinets

A thin metal lip that mounts to the top edge of a drawer or door, so the hardware is almost invisible from the front. Edge pulls keep flat-panel kitchens looking clean and uninterrupted, and they do not catch clothing in a tight galley, one of the reasons our small kitchen page suggests low-profile pulls.

They need a door thick and solid enough to take the screws, and they suit full-overlay and frameless construction best. On lower drawers you are grabbing a small lip, so they suit hands that grip well.

Pros

  • Minimal, modern
  • Nothing sticks out to catch hips or clothing

Watch-outs

  • Small grip surface
  • Not every door material takes them well

6 Integrated Channel (Handleless)

Best for a fully handleless European look

Instead of hardware on the front, a finger channel is built into the top of the drawer or into a recessed rail in the cabinet box, so the fronts are completely flat. This is the cleanest look in cabinetry, and it is specified with the cabinets, not added afterward.

It is typically paired with frameless construction and flat panel doors. Because it is designed into the boxes, it is harder to change later than surface hardware, and the channels need occasional wiping. It is a good choice for a modern kitchen you plan to live with for a long time.

Pros

  • Cleanest possible cabinet face
  • Nothing to snag in narrow aisles

Watch-outs

  • Must be ordered with the cabinets
  • Hard to change later

7 Push-to-Open Hardware

Best for tall panels and appliance-matching fronts

A mechanism inside the cabinet that pops the door or drawer open when you press the front. It gives a handleless look without a channel and works nicely on tall pantry doors, panels that hide appliances, and upper cabinets out of easy reach of a pull.

Push-to-open fronts tend to collect fingerprints where you press, and some mechanisms do not pair with soft-close slides the same way. Ask to try the exact mechanism, and consider it for a few specific cabinets rather than the whole kitchen.

Pros

  • Handleless look on any flat door
  • Useful on tall and panel-ready fronts

Watch-outs

  • Fingerprints at the press point
  • Mechanisms add cost and adjustment

8 Appliance Pulls

Best for panel-ready refrigerators and pantry doors

A longer, sturdier pull designed for heavy doors. Panel-ready refrigerators, which our trends guide lists as a growing 2026 choice, need one that matches the rest of your hardware, as do tall pantry cabinets.

Pick appliance pulls from the same collection as your cabinet pulls so the finish matches exactly; finishes vary between manufacturers even when they share a name. Confirm the mounting requirements with the appliance panel before ordering.

Pros

  • Strong grip on heavy doors
  • Ties panel-ready appliances into the cabinetry

Watch-outs

  • Need to match finish to the cabinet hardware

Our 2026 trends guide notes that brushed nickel dominance is softening, unlacquered and satin brass and warm bronze are gaining, and matte black on everything is cooling. Mixed metals are fine when they are deliberate, for example a matte black faucet with brass cabinet pulls. In hard-water areas around Prescott and the West Valley, finishes near the sink show mineral spots; brushed and satin textures hide them better than polished or flat black.

Sizing and Placement Rules of Thumb

  • Drawers: a pull about one-third the drawer width, centered; very wide drawers get a longer pull or two.
  • Doors: knobs or pulls near the corner opposite the hinge; on base doors near the top, on upper doors near the bottom.
  • Consistency: pick one or two lengths and repeat them, so drawers of similar width match.
  • Test the grip: order one of each and live with them before buying the whole kitchen.

Changing hardware is part of almost every kitchen remodel and every cabinet refinish or reface; new pulls on existing cabinets is also a classic kitchen refresh move. If new hardware needs new holes, plan it with the refinish so old holes can be filled before the finish goes on. For help finding an installer, see our list of top cabinet installers in Prescott.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size cabinet pulls should I use?

A common rule of thumb is a pull about one-third the width of the drawer. On standard doors, a shorter pull or a knob works, and very wide drawers look best with a longer pull or two pulls. Pick one or two lengths and repeat them across the kitchen so the hardware looks consistent.

Should I use knobs or pulls on kitchen cabinets?

Pulls are easier to grip and work on both doors and drawers, so they are the safer choice, especially for anyone with arthritis. A popular approach is knobs on doors and pulls on drawers in one matching finish. Using bar pulls everywhere is the other clean option.

What cabinet hardware finish is in style for 2026?

Warm metals are gaining, including brushed and satin brass, unlacquered brass and warm bronze. Satin nickel stays a safe neutral, and matte black is still used but no longer on everything. Mixing metals is fine when it is deliberate, such as a black faucet with brass pulls.

What hardware is best for seniors or people with arthritis?

Choose bar or D-shaped pulls that you can grab with a whole hand rather than small knobs or tight cup pulls. The 2010 ADA Standards call for operable parts that work with one hand and without tight grasping, pinching or twisting of the wrist, which is a useful guide even in a private home.

Can I change cabinet hardware without replacing my cabinets?

Yes. New pulls and knobs are one of the easiest kitchen updates. If the new hardware uses different hole spacing, the old holes need to be filled and touched up, so it is best to change hardware at the same time as a refinish or reface.
