How to Choose the Best Home Remodeling Contractor in Avondale, AZ

By Steve Hunt, Owner & Lead Designer at Infinity Kitchens and Baths

Reviewed by Steve Hunt, Owner & Lead Designer · AZ ROC #339999

Updated November 11, 2026 • 13 min read

Part of our Choosing a Remodeling Contractor — see all 15 guides in this category.

Quick answer: To choose the best home remodeling contractor in Avondale, AZ: verify an active Arizona ROC license at roc.az.gov, confirm insurance and workers' comp, and favor companies with in-house crews. For multi-room and whole-house work, prioritize a single accountable team and design-build capability over hiring trades piecemeal, plus strong project management, a clear written contract with a defined change-order process, detailed estimates, and a workmanship warranty. Then ask the Avondale questions: who pulls the City of Avondale permit, how HOA approval fits the schedule, and how they plan around summer heat, the monsoon, and very hard water. Infinity Kitchens and Baths (AZ ROC #339999) is family-owned, design-build, and has been remodeling homes since 2011.

Search "best home remodeling contractor in Avondale" and you'll get ads, directories, and a long list of companies all claiming the top spot. A whole-house or multi-room remodel is one of the biggest investments most homeowners make, and the wrong contractor can turn it into months of stress. Drawing on 35+ years in the trade, here's the honest checklist we'd use ourselves to separate a contractor who delivers from one who disappears halfway through — with the Avondale-specific checks (city permits, HOAs, heat and water) that a generic list leaves out.

It applies whether you're redoing one room or the whole house. Most Avondale homes are 1990s and 2000s builds — the median home was built in 2002 — so many are now at the age where kitchens, baths, flooring and fixtures come due at once.

1. Verify the License and Insurance First

In Arizona, remodeling contractors must hold an active license with the Registrar of Contractors (ROC). Before you talk price, ask for the ROC number and look it up at roc.az.gov — it's free and takes a minute. Confirm the license is active and in the right classification, and verify the contractor carries general liability insurance and workers' compensation. If a contractor can't or won't give you a current ROC number, walk away. The City of Avondale's permit application notes that contractors must hold an ROC license unless the homeowner qualifies as an owner-builder, so be wary of anyone who asks you to pull the permit in your own name. (Infinity holds AZ ROC #339999.)

2. Design-Build vs. Hiring Piecemeal

You can hire a designer, then a general contractor, then individual trades — and live in the gaps between them. Or you can choose a design-build firm where one company handles both the design and the construction under a single contract. Design-build closes the gap between a designer who isn't accountable for the budget and a builder who didn't draw the plans. For most homeowners it means fewer surprises, a budget that reflects reality from day one, and one phone number to call.

3. A Single Accountable Team for the Whole Project

For multi-room or whole-house remodels, the biggest risk is coordination. When you hire trades piecemeal, you become the project manager — chasing the plumber, the tiler, and the electrician, each of whom blames the other when the schedule slips. A contractor with a single accountable team sequences the trades, owns the schedule, and answers for the result. That accountability is worth more than any single line item on a bid.

4. Project Management and Communication

The difference between a smooth remodel and a miserable one is usually communication. Ask who your point of contact is, how often you'll get updates, and how decisions and selections are tracked. A contractor with a real project-management process — clear schedules, regular check-ins, and prompt answers — keeps a complex job moving and keeps you from being surprised. If they're slow to respond during the sales process, that rarely improves once the deposit clears.

5. In-House Crews vs. Subcontractors

Ask who actually does the work. Companies that run their own installation crews tend to deliver more consistent quality and accountability than those that hand each phase to a rotating cast of subcontractors. On a whole-house project that spans many trades, in-house crews mean fewer hand-off errors and a team that knows how the whole job fits together.

6. Match the Contractor to the Services You Need

Some remodelers do one thing; a full-service contractor can carry your project from a single room to a whole-home transformation without you having to hire and coordinate multiple companies. Before you sign, make sure the contractor's services actually cover your scope — and ideally a little beyond it, in case the project grows.

Whole-home remodeling

Larger projects — opening up floor plans, remodeling multiple rooms, or updating an entire home — demand real project management and the ability to sequence trades correctly. Aging in place

If you plan to stay in your home for the long haul, or have a parent moving in, aging-in-place features — curbless showers, grab bars, wider doorways, comfort-height fixtures — are worth building in during a remodel. A contractor experienced in accessible design can blend these features so they look intentional, not institutional. The smart move is to add the reinforcement and clearances now, while the walls are already open, even if you don't install every accessory yet; retrofitting later costs far more than building it in during a remodel you're already doing.

Matching the contractor to your scope isn't only about today's project. Homeowners frequently start with one room and, happy with the result, move on to the next. Choosing a full-service contractor from the start means you build a relationship with a company that already knows your home, your finishes, and your preferences — so the second and third projects go faster and match the first.

7. Ask Who Pulls the City of Avondale Permit

Residential permits in Avondale come from the Building Services Division of the city's Development Services Department (11465 W. Civic Center Dr., 623-333-4000), and applications are filed online through the city's Accela portal. Since Jan. 1, 2026, the city enforces the 2024 International Codes and the 2023 National Electrical Code, with local amendments — so ask any contractor whether they're working to the current code.

Not everything needs a permit. Avondale's code lists painting, papering, tiling, carpeting, cabinets, countertops and similar finish work as exempt, and fences and walls up to 6 feet are exempt too (taller block walls need a permit). Plumbing, electrical, gas and HVAC changes beyond minor repairs are not exempt, and neither are structural changes or additions. If your plans include an addition, the city's plan review checklist requires a soils report for all construction containing livable area. Patio covers are permitted and get their own final inspection.

The right answer to "who pulls the permit?" is the contractor, under their ROC license. They should also schedule the inspections: the city inspects Monday through Thursday, wants rough work seen "prior to concealment or cover," and lets a permit expire if no inspection is approved within 180 days. Be wary of anyone who suggests skipping the permit for work that needs one. Our Avondale remodeling permits guide covers the process in more detail.

8. Know Your Avondale House: HOAs, Heat and Hard Water

HOA approval. If your home is in an HOA community, check its rules before you plan any exterior change — a patio cover, a wall, new windows, paint colors — and before a dumpster lands in your driveway. The City of Avondale does not track HOA rules; it tells owners to check with their HOA or title company before coming to the city for a permit. A good contractor builds the HOA review into the schedule instead of discovering it mid-project.

Summer heat and the monsoon. National Weather Service 1991–2020 normals for the area show about 111 days a year at 100°F or hotter, and the monsoon officially runs June 15 to Sept. 30. Interior remodels run year-round, but exterior work — patio covers, concrete, block walls, anything that opens the roof — should be scheduled and protected with the season in mind. Avondale's construction-hours rules allow weekday work from 5 AM between May 1 and Oct. 15 (6 AM the rest of the year) and 7 AM on weekends and holidays, so ask what hours the crew will keep. See the best time to remodel in Avondale for a season-by-season plan.

Very hard water. Avondale's tap water blends city well water with Colorado River water, and the city's 2022 water quality report put city well water at about 21 grains per gallon — "very hard." A whole-house remodel is the time to choose fixtures, faucets and finishes that hold up to mineral buildup, and to plan for a softener or filter if you want one while the plumbing is open. Our guide to faucets and fixtures for hard water has specifics.

Older homes. About one in eight Avondale homes was built before 1980, and the city's oldest neighborhoods include Old Town and Cashion. Older homes can hide outdated materials behind the walls, and a renovation that falls under the asbestos rules needs a notice to Maricopa County 10 business days before work starts. Ask how the contractor handles it.

9. The Remodeling Process, Step by Step

Understanding the sequence helps you spot a contractor who's cutting corners. A well-run remodel generally follows this path:

  1. Consultation & needs assessment — the contractor visits your home, listens to your goals, and discusses budget and priorities honestly.
  2. Design & material selection — layouts, finishes, and fixtures are chosen and priced against your real budget, so there are no surprises later.
  3. Written estimate & contract — you receive a detailed, itemized proposal and a clear contract with scope, schedule, and payment milestones.
  4. HOA review & permitting — any HOA approval is secured, and the contractor pulls the required City of Avondale permits.
  5. Demolition & rough-in — old materials are removed and framing, plumbing, and electrical rough-ins are completed and inspected by the city before walls are closed.
  6. Installation & finishes — cabinets, countertops, tile, flooring, fixtures, and paint are installed by the crew.
  7. Final inspection, walkthrough & warranty — permitted work passes its final inspection, you inspect the work together, a punch list is completed, and warranty terms are confirmed in writing.

Note that design, HOA review, permitting, and material ordering all happen before demolition. A contractor who wants to start swinging hammers before the design is finalized and materials are ordered is inviting delays and change-order surprises.

10. Clear Contracts and a Defined Change-Order Process

Read the contract before you sign. The best remodelers give you a detailed, itemized written estimate — scope, materials, labor, allowances, permits, and timeline — plus a written change-order process so that any mid-project change is documented, priced, and signed off before work proceeds. That's what protects you from surprise charges and keeps the project transparent. Set aside a contingency for what's behind the walls, too; a trustworthy contractor will mention it up front rather than pretending surprises never happen. Also confirm what the workmanship warranty covers and for how long, and look for a substantial track record of reviews and a portfolio of completed homes.

Red Flags to Avoid

  • No ROC number, or one that doesn't check out at roc.az.gov
  • Large upfront cash deposit demands
  • Slow, vague communication during the bidding process
  • Vague, one-line estimates with no scope or timeline
  • No written change-order process
  • Suggesting you skip the permit, or asking you to pull it yourself as an owner-builder
  • Wanting to start demolition before the design, HOA approval, and permits are in place
  • No proof of insurance or workers' compensation

Questions to Ask Before You Sign

  • What's your ROC license number, and are you insured?
  • Do you offer design-build, or just construction?
  • Who is my single point of contact, and how will you keep me updated?
  • Do your own crews do the work, or subcontractors?
  • Which parts of my project need a City of Avondale permit, and will you pull it and schedule the inspections?
  • Does any of this need HOA approval, and how does that fit the schedule?
  • How will you schedule around summer heat and the monsoon?
  • How are change orders documented and priced?
  • What does my written estimate include, and what warranty do you provide?

Frequently Asked Questions

Who is the best home remodeling contractor in Avondale, AZ?

The best home remodeling contractor for you is one that's properly licensed (verify the AZ ROC number at roc.az.gov), insured, uses in-house crews, and gives you a single accountable team for multi-room and whole-house projects. In Avondale, they should also pull the City of Avondale permits your project needs, build HOA approval into the schedule, and plan for summer heat and very hard water. Look for design-build capability, strong project management and communication, clear contracts with a defined change-order process, detailed written estimates, a warranty, and solid reviews. Infinity Kitchens and Baths (AZ ROC #339999) meets each of these standards.

Do I need a permit to remodel my home in Avondale?

It depends on the work. The City of Avondale's building code lists painting, tiling, carpeting, cabinets and countertops as finish work that needs no building permit. Plumbing, electrical, gas and HVAC changes beyond minor repairs do need one, and so do structural changes and additions. Residential permits come from the city's Building Services Division (Development Services Department) and are filed online through the city's Accela portal. Your contractor should pull the permit under their ROC license and schedule the inspections. See our Avondale remodeling permits guide.

Do I need HOA approval for a remodel in Avondale?

If your home is in an HOA community, check its rules before you plan any exterior change, such as a patio cover, wall, windows or paint colors. The City of Avondale does not track HOA rules; it tells owners to check with their HOA or title company before applying for a permit. A good contractor will build the HOA review into the schedule.

What is design-build, and why does it matter?

Design-build means one company handles both the design and the construction under a single contract. It matters because you avoid the gap between a designer who isn't accountable for the budget and a builder who didn't draw the plans. With design-build there's one team responsible for the whole project, which usually means fewer surprises and tighter coordination.

Is it better to hire one contractor or several for a whole-house remodel?

For multi-room or whole-house projects, a single accountable team almost always beats hiring trades piecemeal. One contractor coordinates the schedule, sequences the trades, and owns the result, so you're not the one chasing a plumber, a tiler, and an electrician who each blame the other when something slips.

How should change orders be handled on a remodel?

Changes should be documented in writing before any work proceeds, with the cost and schedule impact spelled out and signed off by both sides. A clear written contract and a defined change-order process protect you from surprise charges and keep the project transparent from start to finish.
