Best Kitchen Layouts for Small Homes (2026)

By Steve Hunt, Owner & Lead Designer at Infinity Kitchens and Baths

Updated September 22, 2026 • 9 min read

Quick answer: For most small homes the best kitchen layout is an L-shape (it opens to the living area and handles a corner well), followed by a well-planned galley when the room is long and narrow and a peninsula when you want seating but lack room for an island. Choose by clearance first: keep at least 42 inches between facing counters in a galley, and only add an island if you can hold at least 36 inches of walkway on every side. See our small kitchen remodeling page for the storage, lighting and appliance side of the job.

In a small home the layout is the one decision you can’t fix later with paint or hardware. It sets where the plumbing and gas run, how many people can work at once, how much counter you get between the sink and the range, and whether the kitchen feels like part of the house or a corridor behind a wall. We redraw a lot of 1970s–1990s Prescott kitchens, and the pattern is familiar: a closed galley or a cramped U that was never planned for two cooks.

This list ranks the layouts we recommend most often for compact footprints, with what each one needs in floor space and where it goes wrong. For the finishing moves that make a small kitchen feel bigger (ceiling-height cabinets, lighter colors, compact appliances, lighting), read our small kitchen remodeling guide. The clearances below are general planning guidelines, not code requirements; your actual room, doors and windows decide the final drawing.

The list

  1. L-Shape
  2. Galley (Two Parallel Runs)
  3. One-Wall
  4. Peninsula (G-Shape)
  5. L-Shape Plus Island
  6. U-Shape
  7. One-Wall Plus Island
  8. Open Galley (Remove One Side Wall)

1 L-Shape

Best all-around layout for small, open homes

Two runs of cabinets meeting at a corner. The L is our default recommendation for small homes because it leaves two sides of the room open, so the kitchen reads as part of the living or dining area instead of a closed box. It naturally forms a tight work triangle with the sink on one leg and the range on the other, and it keeps traffic out of the cook’s path.

The weak spot is the corner. Without a lazy Susan or blind-corner pull-out, that inside corner becomes a dead zone, so plan the corner cabinet at the same time as the layout (our kitchen cabinets page covers the options). Keep the legs balanced if you can; one very long leg and one stub makes the triangle lopsided.

Pros

  • Open on two sides; feels larger
  • Easy work triangle
  • Room to add a small table or peninsula later

Watch-outs

  • Corner storage needs a real solution
  • Less upper storage than a U

2 Galley (Two Parallel Runs)

Best for long, narrow rooms and one main cook

Two facing runs with an aisle between them. Galleys have a bad reputation because so many older ones are dark and closed at both ends, but the layout itself is one of the most efficient ever drawn: every step is short and there is counter on both sides of you. The key number on our small kitchen page is an aisle of at least 42 inches between the two runs so appliance doors can open and you can pass the person at the sink.

Put the sink and range on opposite runs, slightly offset, so two people are not standing back to back at hot and wet stations. If one end of the galley is a wall you can open, that single change usually matters more than any cabinet upgrade (see item 8).

Pros

  • Very short walking distances
  • Maximum counter and base cabinet run for the floor area
  • Cheaper to plumb if you keep the sink wall

Watch-outs

  • Crowded with two cooks unless the aisle is generous
  • Can feel tunnel-like if both ends are closed

3 One-Wall

Best for studios, casitas and ADU conversions

Everything on a single wall: refrigerator, sink, range and cabinets in one line. It uses the least floor area of any layout, which is why we use it in garage conversions and casitas (see garage conversion ADUs). Plumbing and venting all live on one wall, which keeps the rough-in simple.

The trade-off is counter space and walking. There is no triangle, only a line, so put the sink in the middle with the refrigerator at one end and the range at the other, and protect a clear stretch of counter between sink and range for prep. Run the upper cabinets to the ceiling, because you only get one wall of them. If the room allows it later, a one-wall kitchen plus an island (item 7) is the natural upgrade.

Pros

  • Smallest footprint
  • Simplest plumbing and venting
  • Leaves the rest of the room open

Watch-outs

  • Limited counter and storage
  • Long walks if the wall is long

4 Peninsula (G-Shape)

Best way to get seating without room for an island

A peninsula is a counter run attached to the end of an L or U, open on three sides. It gives you most of what people want from an island (a seating edge, more prep space, a place for guests to land) while using less floor, because you only need walkway on the open sides. Our kitchen island guide says it plainly: if you can’t hold 36 inches of clearance all the way around an island, a peninsula is usually the smarter call.

For seating, the same island rules apply: about 12 inches of overhang and roughly 24 inches of width per stool.

Pros

  • Seating and prep space in a small room
  • Needs less clearance than an island
  • Adds base cabinets and drawers

Watch-outs

  • Can create a dead-end if it blocks the entry
  • The inside corner still needs a storage solution

5 L-Shape Plus Island

Best for small homes with an open great room

If your kitchen opens to a family room and you have the floor for it, an L with an island is the most flexible plan there is: the L carries the appliances and the island becomes the prep, seating and serving surface.

It only works if the clearances hold. Per our island guide, keep at least 36 inches of walkway on every side, 42–48 inches where two people pass, a dishwasher or oven door swings, or stools sit, and make the island at least 40 inches deep. If those numbers leave you a tiny island, drop back to a peninsula (item 4) or a cart.

Pros

  • Multiple people can work at once
  • Island adds storage and seating
  • Keeps the cook facing the room

Watch-outs

  • Needs the most floor area on this list
  • Island plumbing or power adds cost and planning

6 U-Shape

Best for storage in a small, enclosed kitchen

Cabinets on three walls. A U gives you the most counter and cabinet run of any layout in a closed room, and the cook stands in the middle with everything within a turn. For a serious cook in a small house, it is hard to beat for storage.

The risk is feeling boxed in. Two inside corners mean two corner cabinets to solve, and in a small U the opposite legs can end up too close together. Hold the same working aisle you would in a galley, and if anyone in the household uses a walker or wheelchair, plan around the turning space in the 2010 ADA Standards (§304.3.1: a 60-inch-diameter circle) so they can turn around inside the U. Our accessible remodeling page covers that planning. Often we open the third wall into a peninsula to get the storage without the enclosure.

Pros

  • Most storage and counter in a closed room
  • Keeps through-traffic out

Watch-outs

  • Two corners to solve
  • Can feel cramped; hard for two cooks in a small U

7 One-Wall Plus Island

Best for narrow open plans and condos

A single appliance wall with a parallel island is really a galley with one side freed from the wall. It suits long, narrow open rooms, which are common in condos, townhomes and some newer builds. The wall keeps tall items (refrigerator, pantry, oven tower) out of sight lines, and the island supplies the counter a one-wall kitchen lacks.

Treat the gap between the wall and the island like a galley aisle: at least 42 inches, more if two cooks share it. Putting the sink or cooktop in the island makes it a true work surface but adds plumbing or venting in the floor, so decide that early; it is one of the choices that locks in once the floor is closed up.

Pros

  • Open to the room on one side
  • Tall storage stays on the back wall

Watch-outs

  • Island utilities add planning
  • Needs a room long enough for both runs

8 Open Galley (Remove One Side Wall)

Best upgrade for closed 1970s–1990s kitchens

Not a new shape so much as a fix: keep the galley or U, but remove the wall that closes it off from the dining or living room and turn that side into a peninsula or island. The workflow stays efficient and the room gains light and sight lines. It is often the single highest-impact change we make in older Prescott homes.

Two cautions. First, some of those walls are load-bearing; that is solvable with an engineered header, but it has to be assessed and permitted (see permit costs in Yavapai County). Second, walls hold cabinets, so you lose upper storage. Our open-concept vs. traditional kitchen comparison explains how a reach-in pantry elsewhere usually makes that up.

Pros

  • Big gain in light and openness
  • Keeps an efficient working core

Watch-outs

  • Possible structural work and permits
  • Less wall cabinet space

How to Choose: The Work Triangle and Clearances

The classic work triangle connects the sink, the cooking surface and the refrigerator. A widely used planning guideline from the kitchen design industry is that each leg of the triangle should run roughly 4 to 9 feet and the three legs together should total no more than about 26 feet, with no cabinet or island cutting through a leg. In a small home the more common problem is a triangle that is too tight, with no landing space beside the range or refrigerator.

The clearances matter more than the triangle. The two numbers we check first on every small kitchen are the ones on our own guides: at least 42 inches between facing runs in a galley, and at least 36 inches on every side of an island, stepping up to 42–48 inches where people pass, sit or open appliance doors. We model the layout in CAD before anything is ordered so you can see those aisles, door swings and landing zones, which is part of how we run a kitchen remodel.

Small-Home Notes for Prescott and the West Valley

Many older kitchens we see in Prescott and Prescott Valley were built for one cook and a closed dining room. If you are planning to age in place, favor layouts with wider aisles and fewer dead-end corners, and put everyday items between hip and shoulder height. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most efficient kitchen layout for a small house?

For most small houses an L-shaped kitchen is the most efficient all-around choice because it forms a tight work triangle and stays open on two sides. In a long, narrow room a galley is even more efficient per square foot, as long as you keep at least 42 inches between the two runs. If you want seating but lack room for an island, add a peninsula.

How much space do you need for a kitchen island?

Plan for at least 36 inches of clear walkway on every side of the island, and 42 to 48 inches on any side where two people pass, stools sit, or a dishwasher or oven door opens. The island itself should be at least 40 inches deep to be useful. If you cannot hold those clearances, a peninsula or a rolling cart is usually the better choice.

Is a galley kitchen a bad layout?

No. A galley is one of the most efficient layouts for one main cook because every step is short and there is counter on both sides. Most complaints come from galleys that are closed at both ends or too narrow. Keeping an aisle of at least 42 inches and opening one end to the dining or living area solves most of those problems.

Should I choose a peninsula or an island in a small kitchen?

Choose an island only if the room can keep at least 36 inches of walkway all the way around it. A peninsula needs clearance on fewer sides, so it fits smaller rooms while still giving you seating, prep space and extra base cabinets. In many small Prescott kitchens a peninsula is the better fit.

Can I change my kitchen layout without moving plumbing?

Sometimes. Moving from a U to an L, or opening a galley into a peninsula, can often keep the sink where it is. Moving the sink or range to an island means new plumbing, gas or venting in the floor, which adds planning, cost and sometimes permits.
