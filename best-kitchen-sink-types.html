Best Kitchen Sink Types (2026)

By Steve Hunt, Owner & Lead Designer at Infinity Kitchens and Baths

Updated September 22, 2026 • 9 min read

Quick answer: For most kitchens, the best sink is a large single-bowl undermount in brushed stainless steel: it is easy to clean, fits sheet pans and big pots, and its satin finish hides the hard-water spots common in Prescott and the West Valley. A workstation sink is the best upgrade for serious cooks, a farmhouse (apron-front) sink is the best statement piece, and granite composite is the best pick for a quiet, scratch-resistant bowl, as long as you choose a lighter or speckled color.

The sink is the hardest-working spot in the kitchen, and in Arizona it has an extra job: living with hard water. Prescott and West Valley water leaves calcium and magnesium spots wherever it air-dries, so the finish and color of your sink matter more here than they do in soft-water towns. A glossy black bowl can look chalky within a week.

The sink also has to be decided early. Undermount and farmhouse sinks shape the countertop cutout and the base cabinet, so we pick the sink during design, before the cabinets are ordered and the countertops are templated. Here are the ten sink types we install most, ranked for everyday use in Arizona kitchens.

The list

  1. Undermount Stainless Steel (Single Bowl)
  2. Workstation Sink
  3. Farmhouse (Apron-Front) Sink
  4. Granite Composite
  5. Fireclay
  6. Double Bowl
  7. Integrated Solid-Surface or Quartz Sink
  8. Enameled Cast Iron
  9. Drop-In (Top-Mount)
  10. Copper

1 Undermount Stainless Steel (Single Bowl)

Best overall for most kitchens

An undermount sink mounts beneath the countertop, so there is no rim to catch crumbs; you wipe straight from the counter into the bowl. Paired with a single large basin, it fits roasting pans and sheet pans that never fit in a divided sink. It is the configuration we recommend most, and it is one that never looks dated.

For hard water, choose a brushed or satin finish rather than mirror-polished stainless; the fine grain hides water spots and light scratches. A thicker-gauge steel with sound-deadening pads is quieter and resists dents. Undermount sinks need a stone, quartz, or solid-surface counter; the fabricator cuts and polishes the opening in the shop, which our countertop cost guide lists at $150–$300.

MountUnder the counter
Hard-water lookBrushed finish hides spots
CounterStone, quartz, or solid surface

Pros

  • Easy to wipe crumbs in
  • Fits large pans
  • Timeless

Watch-outs

  • Not for standard laminate counters

2 Workstation Sink

Best for serious home cooks

A workstation sink has a built-in ledge that holds sliding accessories: a cutting board, colander, drying rack, or roll-up mat. You can prep vegetables, rinse, and drain in one spot, which effectively adds counter space in a smaller kitchen.

Most are stainless undermounts, and they tend to be long, so they usually need a wider sink base cabinet. That is easy to plan in a new layout and harder to retrofit into existing cabinets. Keep the accessories in mind when you choose a faucet; a pull-down with enough reach to cover the full bowl is worth it. For a small kitchen, see our small kitchen remodeling ideas.

MountUsually undermount
CabinetOften needs a wider base

Pros

  • Adds usable prep space
  • Accessories store in the sink

Watch-outs

  • Needs a wide base cabinet
  • More parts to clean

3 Farmhouse (Apron-Front) Sink

Best statement sink

A farmhouse sink has an exposed front panel and a deep single bowl. It is the centerpiece of farmhouse, craftsman, and transitional kitchens, and the deep bowl hides a pile of dishes. Because you stand right against the sink, many people find it easier on the back than reaching over a counter edge.

It requires a dedicated apron-front base cabinet, and heavier materials need extra support underneath, so this is a decision to make during cabinet design, not after. Farmhouse sinks come in fireclay, stainless, enameled cast iron, and composite. In hard-water homes, a white fireclay apron shows the least spotting of the bunch. Pair it with a two-tone look from our two-tone cabinet guide.

MountApron-front base cabinet
MaterialsFireclay, stainless, cast iron, composite

Pros

  • Striking focal point
  • Deep, roomy bowl

Watch-outs

  • Needs a special base cabinet
  • Hard to swap later

4 Granite Composite

Best for quiet, scratch-resistant bowls

Granite composite sinks are made from crushed stone bound with resin. They are quieter than stainless, very scratch resistant, and handle heat well, and the color runs through the material so light scuffs do not show.

The Arizona caveat is color. Hard water can leave a whitish mineral film on dark composite, especially solid black, and it takes regular wiping to keep it looking deep and even. Lighter grays, beiges, and speckled colors hide mineral residue far better. Match the sink to your counter for a calm, built-in look; a gray composite under a gray-veined quartz almost disappears.

MountUndermount or drop-in
Hard-water lookChoose light or speckled colors

Pros

  • Quiet
  • Resists scratches and chips
  • Heat tolerant

Watch-outs

  • Dark colors show mineral film

5 Fireclay

Best for a classic white sink

Fireclay is clay fired at very high temperatures with a glazed surface. The glaze is glossy, nonporous, and easy to clean, and white fireclay is the best-looking sink we know of for a hard-water home, because mineral spots barely show on white.

Fireclay is heavy and can chip or crack if you drop a cast-iron pan into it, so a bottom grid is a good idea. It is most often sold as a farmhouse sink, and like any heavy sink it needs solid cabinet support. If you want a bright, traditional look that stays clean with a quick wipe, fireclay is hard to beat.

SurfaceGlazed, nonporous
Hard-water lookWhite hides spots well

Pros

  • Easy-clean glaze
  • Hides mineral spots in white

Watch-outs

  • Heavy
  • Can chip from dropped cookware

6 Double Bowl

Best for hand-washing and multitasking

A double-bowl sink lets you soak in one side and rinse in the other, or keep dirty dishes out of sight while you prep in the second bowl. It suits households that hand-wash a lot or have two cooks working at once.

The catch is that neither bowl fits a large pan lying flat. An offset double, with one larger bowl and one smaller prep bowl, or a low-divide design that lets water pass over the middle, keeps most of the flexibility of a single bowl. If you run the dishwasher daily, a single large bowl is usually the better choice.

LayoutsEqual, offset, or low-divide

Pros

  • Soak and rinse at once
  • Good for two cooks

Watch-outs

  • Big pans do not lie flat

7 Integrated Solid-Surface or Quartz Sink

Best seamless look

An integrated sink is made from the same material as the countertop, or fabricated to join it with no visible seam. With no rim and no joint, there is nowhere for grime or mineral buildup to collect, which makes it one of the easiest sinks to keep clean in hard water.

Solid surface is the classic choice here, and scratches can usually be sanded out. The drawback is that sink and counter are one unit, so if the bowl is ever damaged, repair is a fabricator's job rather than a simple sink swap. It is a great fit for modern and minimalist kitchens.

SeamsNone or nearly invisible
RepairBy a fabricator

Pros

  • No rim to clean
  • Clean, modern look

Watch-outs

  • Harder to replace the bowl alone

8 Enameled Cast Iron

Best for durability with a vintage look

Enameled cast iron is extremely durable and very quiet, with a glossy enamel finish that comes in white and a range of colors. A well-kept cast-iron sink can last for decades.

It is very heavy and needs a reinforced base, and the enamel can chip if something heavy is dropped on it, exposing dark iron underneath. Abrasive cleaners can dull the finish, so use soft cleaners for hard-water spots. It suits a vintage or traditional kitchen and is available in both drop-in and undermount versions.

WeightVery heavy
FinishGlossy enamel

Pros

  • Very durable and quiet
  • Classic look

Watch-outs

  • Heavy
  • Enamel can chip

9 Drop-In (Top-Mount)

Best for laminate counters and budget updates

A drop-in sink sits into a cutout from above, with its rim resting on the counter. It is the easiest sink to install and replace, and it is the standard choice for laminate countertops, which usually cannot support an undermount.

The rim is its weakness: crumbs cannot be wiped straight into the bowl, and the joint between rim and counter collects grime and hard-water crust. If you are replacing counters with quartz or stone anyway, most clients choose an undermount instead. For a quick refresh on existing laminate, a drop-in is a sensible, budget-friendly fix.

MountRim on top of the counter
CounterAny, including laminate

Pros

  • Easy to install and replace
  • Works with laminate

Watch-outs

  • Rim traps grime and mineral crust

10 Copper

Best for a living-finish look

Copper sinks bring warmth to rustic and Southwest kitchens and pair well with warm-metal fixtures. Copper develops a patina over time that many people love.

That patina is the point, and it is also the maintenance. Acidic foods and hard water change the surface color unevenly, and keeping copper bright takes regular polishing. If you want a sink that always looks new, choose something else. If you like a finish that changes with the house, copper is a characterful choice.

UpkeepHigher if you want it bright

Pros

Planning Your Sink in a Remodel

Decide before cabinets and counters. Farmhouse and workstation sinks change the base cabinet, and every undermount changes the countertop cutout. We choose the sink during design so the cabinet, counter, and plumbing all line up.

Think twice before moving it. Relocating a sink means rerouting drain and supply lines; our kitchen remodel cost guide budgets $800 to $2,500 for a sink relocation, depending on distance and access.

Match the faucet to the water. Brushed and satin faucet finishes hide hard-water spots better than polished chrome or matte black. Wiping the sink and faucet dry at night does more against mineral buildup than any cleaner.

We install sinks as part of every kitchen remodel, along with the countertops they sit in. If you are comparing contractors, see our lists of top kitchen remodelers in Prescott and top kitchen remodelers in Peoria and Glendale.

Frequently Asked Questions

What type of kitchen sink is easiest to keep clean?

An undermount or integrated sink is easiest, because there is no rim for crumbs and mineral crust to collect in. A brushed stainless or white fireclay bowl hides hard-water spots best. Drop-in sinks are the hardest to keep clean because of the joint between the rim and the counter.

Is a single or double bowl kitchen sink better?

For most households with a dishwasher, a single large bowl is better because it fits sheet pans and big pots. A double bowl suits people who hand-wash a lot or cook with a partner. An offset or low-divide double bowl is a good middle ground.

Do granite composite sinks show hard water spots?

Dark composite sinks, especially solid black, can show a whitish mineral film in hard-water areas like Prescott and the West Valley. Lighter grays, beiges, and speckled colors hide mineral residue much better. Wiping the sink dry after use helps any color.

Can I put an undermount sink in a laminate countertop?

Usually not. Standard laminate countertops are built for drop-in sinks, because the exposed edge of an undermount cutout would let water into the core. If you want an undermount, choose quartz, granite, quartzite, or solid surface for the counter.

Does a farmhouse sink need a special cabinet?

Yes. A farmhouse or apron-front sink needs a dedicated base cabinet with a cut-down front, and heavier fireclay or cast iron models need extra support underneath. That is why we choose the sink during cabinet design rather than after the cabinets are installed.
