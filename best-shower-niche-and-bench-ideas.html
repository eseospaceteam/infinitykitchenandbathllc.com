Best Shower Niche and Bench Ideas (2026)

By Steve Hunt, Owner & Lead Designer at Infinity Kitchens and Baths

Updated September 22, 2026 • 9 min read

Quick answer: The best shower niche is one recessed niche, built between the studs on a wall you do not face under the spray, with its bottom shelf pitched toward the shower so water drains out. The best bench depends on the room: a built-in tiled bench in a shower of about 36×60 inches or larger, a floating bench where you want an open floor, and a fold-down seat where space is tight. Plan both before demolition, because both live inside the waterproofing. See our walk-in showers page for how we build them.

Niches and benches are the two shower details people touch every day, and the two that most often get improvised once the walls are open. A niche added as an afterthought ends up where the stud happened to be, not where your hand goes. A bench bolted on later means a seat that sits in the spray or blocks the door.

Below are the niche and bench ideas we recommend most in Prescott and West Valley remodels, ordered roughly by how much they improve daily use. Each one notes where it fits and what to watch for. The dimensions we give are the same ones we publish on our tile shower ideas and aging-in-place bathroom guides, so the numbers line up across the site.

The list

  1. One Large Recessed Niche Instead of Several Small Ones
  2. Niche on a Side Wall, Out of the Direct Spray
  3. Size the Niche to the Stud Bay
  4. Pitched Niche Floor So Water Drains Out
  5. Built-In Tiled Bench
  6. Floating Bench
  7. Corner Bench or Corner Shelf
  8. Fold-Down Shower Seat
  9. Accent-Tile or Lit Niche
  10. Niche Built Into a Groutless Panel Shower

1 One Large Recessed Niche Instead of Several Small Ones

Best for most showers

If you only do one thing, do this. A single niche of roughly 12×24 inches up to 16×32 inches holds more than two or three small ones, and it is easier to waterproof well because there are fewer inside corners to seal. Add a shelf inside the opening and you get two tiers of storage from one cut in the wall.

Size it to what you actually keep in the shower. Tall pump bottles need a taller opening than bar soap and a razor. We usually ask clients to line up their current bottles on the counter during the consultation so we can size the niche to them rather than to a stock number.

Typical size12×24 in. to 16×32 in. (from our tile shower ideas guide)
ShelvesOne shelf inside the opening doubles the tiers

Pros

  • More usable storage per square inch
  • Fewer corners to waterproof and clean

Watch-outs

  • A wide niche may need a framed header (see #3)

2 Niche on a Side Wall, Out of the Direct Spray

Best placement for keeping bottles clean

The most common placement mistake is putting the niche on the wall directly across from the showerhead, where it catches the full spray. Bottles stay wet, soap residue builds up, and in our hard water the mineral film on the niche floor shows up quickly.

A side wall, or the valve wall offset from the head, keeps the niche reachable without soaking it. Set the bottom shelf somewhere between elbow and chest height for the main user so nobody bends to reach shampoo. If someone in the house sits to shower, add a second, lower niche within reach of the bench.

Also avoid exterior walls where you can. Cutting into an insulated outside wall leaves a thin spot for condensation, and that is not where you want a pocket of trapped moisture.

Pros

  • Bottles dry between showers
  • Easy reach without bending

Watch-outs

  • Needs a check for plumbing and vent lines in that wall

3 Size the Niche to the Stud Bay

Best for keeping cost and framing simple

Most interior walls are framed with studs 16 inches on center, which leaves roughly 14½ inches of clear width between them, and a standard 2×4 wall gives about 3½ inches of depth. A niche that fits inside one bay needs only blocking at the top and bottom. That is why a 12-inch-wide niche is the easy default.

Wider niches are fine, but they mean cutting a stud and framing a header, which is more work and must be done correctly on a bearing wall. It is worth doing for a long horizontal niche that runs the width of the shower. It is not worth doing for a few extra inches.

Building the niche into the framing during the remodel is far cleaner than adding a surface caddy later. We confirm stud locations, plumbing, and wiring during demolition before we commit to a size.

Stud spacing16 in. on center is common framing practice
Clear widthAbout 14½ in. between standard studs
DepthAbout 3½ in. in a 2×4 wall

4 Pitched Niche Floor So Water Drains Out

Best for preventing hidden water damage

A niche with a dead-flat bottom collects water. That water sits against the grout, soaks the edges of the tile, and slowly tests the waterproofing underneath. The fix is simple: pitch the niche floor slightly toward the shower, at least 1/8 inch per foot, so every drop runs back out.

This is one of the details most often missed, and it causes water damage over time when it is skipped. The same logic applies to the top of a bench and to any shelf ledge. The shower floor itself gets a steeper slope to the drain (we build to a 1/4-inch-per-foot pitch), but horizontal surfaces up the wall need their own slope too.

Ask any installer how they slope the niche sill. If the answer is a shrug, keep looking.

Niche floor pitch1/8 in. per foot minimum toward the shower
Shower floor pitch1/4 in. per foot to the drain

5 Built-In Tiled Bench

Best for primary baths and long-term use

A built-in bench is the sturdiest seat you can put in a shower, and when it is tiled to match the walls it looks like part of the architecture rather than an accessory. It is where you shave your legs, sit for a steam, or rest if your knees or balance change later.

The comfortable range is about 17 to 19 inches high and 15 to 18 inches deep, and a bench typically takes 24 to 36 inches of one wall. In showers of about 36×60 inches or larger, a bench usually fits without meaningfully shrinking standing room. In anything smaller, look at #6 or #7 instead.

The bench is framed or formed, then wrapped in the same waterproofing as the walls, with its top pitched toward the drain.

HeightAbout 17–19 in.
DepthAbout 15–18 in.
Wall lengthRoughly 24–36 in.

Pros

  • Very solid
  • Looks integrated
  • Easy to clean when tiled with few grout lines

Watch-outs

  • Permanent footprint
  • Needs careful waterproofing on every face

6 Floating Bench

Best for curbless showers and open floors

A floating bench cantilevers off the wall with open floor underneath. The shower floor stays continuous, which is easier to clean and reads larger, and it pairs well with curbless, zero-entry showers where the whole point is an uninterrupted floor.

The support has to be planned in the framing. Floating benches rely on solid blocking or a manufactured bracket system anchored into the wall structure before the waterproofing goes on. This is not something to add after the tile is set. Give the top the same slight pitch as a built-in bench so water does not sit on it.

Pros

  • Open floor under the seat
  • Modern look

Watch-outs

  • Support must be built in before waterproofing
  • Usually a bit less deep than a built-in

7 Corner Bench or Corner Shelf

Best for smaller showers

A triangular corner bench uses the one spot in the shower you rarely stand in. It gives you somewhere to prop a foot or set a bottle without eating a full wall, and in a tub-to-shower conversion it is often the only seat that fits.

A corner bench is not as comfortable for sitting as a full bench, so be honest about how you will use it. If it is mainly a foot rest and a shelf, a corner unit is a smart use of space. If someone needs to sit to bathe, go with a built-in or fold-down seat that gives real support. Groutless panel systems also offer corner shelves that bond to the walls, which is a tidy alternative in a small panel shower.

Pros

  • Uses dead corner space
  • Works in compact showers

Watch-outs

  • Smaller seat than a straight bench

8 Fold-Down Shower Seat

Best for tight showers and aging in place

A fold-down teak or thermoplastic seat adds almost no footprint when folded, but lets the shower be used standing or seated. It is the answer when a permanent bench would crowd the room, and it reads as a spa feature rather than a medical device.

The key is what is behind the wall. A fold-down seat must be anchored into solid blocking installed before the walls close, rated for the load the manufacturer specifies. Pair it with a handheld shower on a slide bar within reach of the seat and a grab bar near it. Our aging-in-place bathroom guide covers seat, grab bar, and handheld placement together.

Pros

  • Near-zero footprint folded
  • Flexible for every user in the house

Watch-outs

  • Needs in-wall blocking planned early
  • Less of a design feature than tile

9 Accent-Tile or Lit Niche

Best for a design focal point

The back of a niche is a small, contained surface, which makes it a good place to splurge. A glass or stone mosaic, a metal-look tile, or a contrasting color frames the niche and signals intent. Because the area is small, a pricier tile barely moves the budget. More ideas are on our tile shower ideas page.

Align the niche with your grout lines so it looks built-in rather than cut-in. That means locking the tile layout before framing the niche, not after. Built-in niche lighting is also popular, but any light in a wet location has to be rated for it and wired to code, so it gets decided during rough-in with the electrician.

Pros

  • High impact for a small amount of tile

Watch-outs

  • Lighting must be wet-rated and planned at rough-in

10 Niche Built Into a Groutless Panel Shower

Best for the lowest-maintenance shower

If you are choosing a groutless shower system, you do not have to give up a niche. Solid-surface and composite panel systems can include niche walls, niche inserts, and corner shelving that integrate with the panels, so the niche has the same smooth, non-porous finish as the rest of the shower.

That matters in our area. Hard water leaves mineral film on everything, and a panel niche with no grout lines wipes clean in seconds. The trade-off is that size and shape follow what the panel system offers, so a niche sized to your exact bottles is easier to get in tile. Compare the two approaches on our groutless vs. tile shower page.

Pros

  • No grout to scrub in the niche
  • Matches the panel finish

Watch-outs

  • Fewer size and shape options than a tiled niche

How We Build Them

Every niche and bench on this list lives inside the shower’s waterproofing, so the order of work matters more than the finish. At Infinity Kitchens and Baths we lock the layout at design, frame niches and benches during rough-in, apply a continuous waterproofing membrane over every face, and only then set tile or panels. That sequence is the same whether it is a custom tile shower or a panel system. If you are comparing remodelers, our list of top bathroom remodelers in Prescott covers what to ask about waterproofing before you sign.

Frequently Asked Questions

How big should a shower niche be?

For most showers, one niche of roughly 12 by 24 inches up to 16 by 32 inches works better than several small ones. A 12-inch-wide niche fits inside a standard stud bay, which keeps framing simple. Add a shelf inside the opening for two tiers of storage, and size the height to your tallest bottles.

Where is the best place to put a shower niche?

On a side wall or offset from the showerhead, not directly across from the spray, so bottles do not stay soaked. Set the bottom shelf between elbow and chest height for the main user. Avoid exterior walls when possible, and check for plumbing and vent lines before cutting.

Should a shower niche be sloped?

Yes. The bottom of the niche should be pitched toward the shower at least 1/8 inch per foot so water drains out instead of pooling against the grout. A flat niche floor is one of the most common details that leads to slow water damage. Bench tops need the same slight pitch.

How high and deep should a shower bench be?

A comfortable built-in shower bench is about 17 to 19 inches high and 15 to 18 inches deep, and it typically takes 24 to 36 inches of one wall. In a shower of about 36 by 60 inches or larger, a bench usually fits without cramping standing space.

Can I add a niche or bench to an existing shower?

Only by opening the wall and redoing the waterproofing in that area, which in practice usually means retiling at least that wall. That is why we plan niches and benches before demolition during a shower or bathroom remodel. A fold-down seat also needs blocking inside the wall, which is far easier to install while the wall is open.
