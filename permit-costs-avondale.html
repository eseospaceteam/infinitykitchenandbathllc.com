Permits — City of Avondale, AZ

Avondale Remodeling Permits: What Needs One

By Steve Hunt, Owner & Lead Designer at Infinity Kitchens and Baths

Reviewed by Steve Hunt, Owner & Lead Designer · AZ ROC #339999

Which remodeling projects need a City of Avondale permit, which don’t, how inspections work, and why skipping one is a serious risk — explained by a licensed Arizona remodeling contractor. Updated November 11, 2026.

Part of our Remodeling Process & Planning — see all 7 guides in this category.

Most kitchen and bathroom remodels in Avondale touch at least one thing that needs a building permit — a moved drain, a new circuit, a gas line, a wall. Some updates need none at all: Avondale’s code exempts painting, tiling, carpeting, cabinets and countertops. Knowing which side of that line your project falls on tells you how much paperwork, how many inspections and how much lead time to expect. And a skipped permit rarely saves anything: the real cost shows up years later, at resale, at insurance claim time, or when unpermitted work fails and nobody is accountable. Here’s what you need to know.

Who Issues Remodeling Permits in Avondale

Residential permits in Avondale come from the Building Services Division of the city’s Development Services Department:

  • Office: 11465 W. Civic Center Dr., Avondale, AZ 85323
  • Phone: (623) 333-4000
  • Hours: Monday through Thursday, 7 AM to 6 PM
  • Applications: filed online through the city’s Accela Citizen Access portal (an account is required); fees are paid through the Avondale Citizen Portal
  • Codes: the 2024 International Codes (including the IRC, IBC, IPC, IMC, IFGC and IECC) and the 2023 National Electrical Code, with city amendments, effective January 1, 2026

Remodels are filed as residential “Alterations/Remodels” permits, and plumbing, electrical and HVAC work goes under “MPE.” The application has separate boxes for an interior alteration or remodel, plumbing, electrical and mechanical work, so one kitchen project can involve several trades on the same permit.

One caution: a ZIP code alone doesn’t settle which office has jurisdiction. Avondale’s main ZIP codes are 85323 and 85392, but a small number of parcels inside them sit in unincorporated county islands, and some Avondale homes use the 85353 or 85340 ZIP codes. We confirm jurisdiction for your address before anything is filed — you never have to figure it out yourself.

What Doesn’t Need a Permit in Avondale

Avondale’s amended version of the residential code (IRC R105.2) lists work that is exempt from a permit. The items that matter most for a remodel:

  • Finish work: “painting, papering, tiling, carpeting, cabinets, counter tops, and similar finish work”
  • Electrical: replacing lamps, receptacles or same-size breakers
  • Gas: portable appliances and minor parts only
  • Plumbing: stopping leaks, clearing stoppages, and pulling and resetting a toilet, “provided such repairs do not involve or require the replacement or rearrangement of valves, pipes, or fixtures”
  • Outside: fences and walls up to 6 ft (Avondale lowered this from the model code’s 7 ft), retaining walls up to 4 ft with no surcharge, sidewalks and driveways, one-story detached accessory structures up to 200 sq ft, and detached decks up to 200 sq ft and 30 in. high

No permit doesn’t mean no rules — exempt work still has to be done to code. And driveway or sidewalk work that reaches into the public right-of-way (curbs, sidewalks, driveway approaches) needs its own separate permit.

Two items people often miss. Water heaters, gas line extensions and like-for-like equipment change-outs appear on the city’s list of minor plumbing, electrical and mechanical work reviewed over the counter — that means a quicker review, not an exemption. And outdoor kitchens aren’t named anywhere in the city’s documents, but new gas piping and new circuits aren’t on the exempt list, so expect a permit for those parts of the job. How the Avondale Permit Process Works

  1. Check your HOA first. “The city does not track restrictive covenants. Be sure to check with your HOA or title company… before coming to the city for a permit.” Exterior changes are the most likely to need HOA approval.
  2. Define the scope. Which trades are involved — plumbing, electrical, gas, mechanical, structural — decides which boxes get checked on the application and which inspections follow.
  3. Apply online. The application is filed through Accela Citizen Access with drawings where the scope calls for them. Projects that add livable space need a soils report: the city’s plan review checklist says a “Soils Report [is] Required for all construction containing livable area.”
  4. Pay the fees through the Avondale Citizen Portal once the permit is ready to issue.
  5. Build and inspect. Each stage is inspected before it’s covered, ending with final inspections.

Who can do the work. The city’s permit application says contractors must hold an Arizona Registrar of Contractors (ROC) license unless the owner qualifies as an owner-builder under A.R.S. 32-1121. Infinity Kitchens and Baths is licensed, bonded and insured under AZ ROC #339999.

Asbestos notice. The same application notes that regulated renovation requires an asbestos NESHAP notice to Maricopa County 10 business days before work starts. Older building materials can contain asbestos, and about 12% of Avondale homes were built before 1980 (U.S. Census Bureau, American Community Survey 2020–2024), so on an older home — around Old Town or in Cashion, for example — ask about this before demolition is scheduled.

Inspections: What to Expect

The city’s residential inspection list includes layout and setbacks, footing/Ufer, pre-slab, post-tension slab, under-slab plumbing and electrical, rough plumbing, electrical, mechanical and framing, pre-drywall insulation, a pre-slope inspection and shower liner test, drywall and lath nailing, and finals for plumbing, electrical, mechanical, building and gas — plus final fence and final patio cover inspections. A typical kitchen remodel sees the rough-in and final inspections for the trades involved; a shower rebuild adds the pre-slope and liner test before tile goes on.

  • Request an inspection by text at (833) 324-1348 or by phone at 623-333-4005.
  • Timing: requests made by 3 PM get next-business-day service. Inspections run Monday through Thursday, 7 AM to 4 PM.
  • Before cover: work must be inspected “prior to concealment or cover” — no drywall over unapproved rough-ins, no tile over an untested shower liner.
  • Don’t let it lapse: a permit expires if no inspection is approved within 180 days.

Construction Work Hours

The permit application also sets working hours: weekdays from 5 AM to 7 PM between May 1 and October 15, and 6 AM to 7 PM between October 16 and April 30; weekends and holidays from 7 AM to 7 PM. The early summer start helps crews get outdoor tasks done before the worst of the heat — see our guide to the best time to remodel in Avondale.

What Permits Cost in Avondale

Permit fees are set by the City of Avondale and depend on the type and scope of the work, so we don’t publish fee estimates on this page — a number we can’t verify isn’t a number you should budget on. On our projects, permit fees are included in your written estimate, along with the time it takes to apply and pass inspections. If you’re comparing bids, check that each one includes permits; a bid that leaves them out isn’t comparing like with like. If you’re managing a project yourself, ask the Building Services Division for the fee on your specific scope before you apply.

What Happens If You Don’t Pull a Permit

Skipping a permit is a short-term cost save with significant long-term consequences. These risks aren’t theoretical:

Resale disclosure. Arizona law requires sellers to disclose material defects, which includes unpermitted work that required a permit. When a buyer’s home inspector flags an unpermitted electrical panel upgrade or an addition with no permit history, it becomes a negotiating lever — price reduction, escrow holdback, or deal collapse. The cost of resolving unpermitted work before closing typically far exceeds the original permit fee.

Insurance claim denial. If a fire or water damage event occurs in an area with unpermitted work, your homeowner’s insurance carrier may deny the claim on the basis that the non-code work contributed to the loss. This is especially relevant for unpermitted electrical work, which is a leading cause of residential fires.

Retroactive permitting. If discovered — by a neighbor complaint, a later permit application, or a home inspection — you may be required to permit the work after the fact. Because Avondale requires inspection before work is concealed, that can mean opening walls or pulling up tile so the work can be seen, failing inspections, and paying for corrections. It costs more and disrupts more than doing it right the first time.

Liability. If unpermitted work injures someone — a structural change that was never inspected fails, an electrical circuit installed without inspection shorts and causes a fire — the homeowner bears liability without the protection of a permitted and inspected installation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a permit for a kitchen remodel in Avondale?

It depends on scope. Avondale’s building code lists painting, tiling, carpeting, cabinets and countertops as finish work that needs no building permit, so replacing cabinets and countertops in place doesn’t need one. Moving a sink or drain, adding circuits, changing a gas line or removing a wall does need a permit from the City of Avondale’s Building Services Division.

Do I need a permit to remodel a bathroom in Avondale?

Retiling, painting and a new vanity cabinet and countertop are exempt finish work. Plumbing is different: the city’s plumbing exemption covers only stopping leaks, clearing stoppages and pulling and resetting a toilet, as long as no valves, pipes or fixtures are replaced or rearranged. Rebuilding a shower, moving a drain or valve, or adding a circuit or fan needs a permit, and shower work gets a pre-slope inspection and a shower liner test.

How do I apply for a building permit in Avondale?

Applications are filed online through the city’s Accela Citizen Access portal, which requires an account. The Building Services Division is at 11465 W. Civic Center Dr., (623) 333-4000, open Monday through Thursday, 7 AM to 6 PM. When we do your remodel, we prepare and file the application for you.

How much does a remodeling permit cost in Avondale?

Fees are set by the city and depend on the type and scope of the work, so we don’t publish estimates here. On our projects, permit fees are included in your written estimate. For an exact figure on a project you’re managing yourself, ask the Building Services Division before you apply.

How do inspections work in Avondale?

Text (833) 324-1348 or call 623-333-4005 to request one. Requests made by 3 PM get next-business-day service, and inspections run Monday through Thursday, 7 AM to 4 PM. Work must be inspected before it is covered, and a permit expires if no inspection is approved within 180 days.

Does my HOA have to approve my remodel?

Check with your HOA before you apply. The City of Avondale does not track restrictive covenants and tells owners to check with their HOA or title company before coming to the city for a permit. Exterior changes are the most likely to need HOA approval.

Can a homeowner pull their own permit in Arizona?

Sometimes. The city’s permit application says contractors must hold an Arizona Registrar of Contractors license unless the owner qualifies as an owner-builder under A.R.S. 32-1121. If you act as your own builder, you take on responsibility for the work, the inspections and the code compliance. Using a licensed contractor means the permit is tied to the contractor’s license, which gives you an additional layer of accountability and protection.

What happens if I skip a permit in Avondale?

Under the building codes Avondale enforces, the building official can stop work that is under way without a permit, and work that was covered without inspection may have to be opened up and corrected. The bigger costs usually come later: problems at resale, a denied insurance claim, or paying to permit and fix the work after the fact.
